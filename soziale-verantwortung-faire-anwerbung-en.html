Responsibility That Starts Before the Flight and Stays After Arrival

Recruiting a nurse takes months. Their path starts much earlier: with the training in their home country and what it costs. Here is what we do there and in Germany.

Where the Path Begins

In Germany, nursing trainees receive a salary. In the Philippines and in India, it looks different. Training there is often very expensive. Many families go into debt for it. Others never start their studies at all.

Even for the clinical placements that the curriculum requires, students in the Philippines pay tuition. They work and pay for it.

A nurse who comes to Germany has often invested many years and a lot of money in their training. That deserves respect.

What We Do in the Home Country

Behind every nurse stand family, school and community. That is why, in the country of origin, we look at the whole community and do not stop at placement.

Preparation begins before departure. Language courses up to B2 run through Lingoda, and Heinz organises them. On top of that come sessions on daily life and work in Germany and an open conversation about expectations before entry. Nurses pay nothing for this. Language costs that someone covered themselves beforehand, we reimburse retroactively for up to one year.

A Scholarship for Nursing Students

In 2027, we are launching a scholarship programme with the Kolehiyo ng Lungsod ng Dasmariñas (KLD). It is for nursing students who are financially disadvantaged. Their education should not depend on money.

The programme grows in stages. The goal is a five-figure amount over the coming years. The students enter into no obligations. We do not use the programme to get early access to skilled nurses.

The HEINZ Model: Arriving and Staying

Recruiting is the smaller part. Arriving decides whether someone stays. That is what the HEINZ model is for. It was developed with students and professors of Leuphana University and has been refined in practice since 2018. We look at integration comprehensively and ground it in research.

The HEINZ Model at a Glance

Phase 0
Initiation. Needs, expectations and framework are clarified together in advance.
H
Handlungssicherheit (confidence to act). Orientation and onboarding from day one.
E
Matching expectations. Transparency about position, team and location.
I
Intercultural understanding and respect. Coaching for employers and teams.
N
Navigation. Language and system, from clinical vocabulary to the authorities.
Z
Future and roots. Settling in locally and career prospects.
Foundation
Leadership. A named contact at the employer for retention, stay interviews after 6, 12 and 24 months and a team that is present day to day.

In daily life, that means help with finding housing, dealing with authorities, opening a bank account, doctor visits, intercultural training, mentoring and local networking. Employers find details on the For Employers page.

Our Commitments: The RAL Quality Mark

Heinz holds the RAL quality mark “Faire Anwerbung Pflege Deutschland”, awarded by GAPA. For it, we committed in writing to six guiding principles. Here they are in our words:

The Six Guiding Principles

Written form
All agreements with nurses and employers are in writing.
Free of charge
Nurses pay nothing for recruitment and placement. The employer bears the costs.
Limiting economic risk
The economic risk of recruitment lies with the employer.
Transparency
Terms, costs and process are open in advance. Payment comes in two steps: 50% after placement, 50% after the start.
Sustainability and participation
We support nurses beyond arrival. They can give feedback, including through the complaint form on this website.
Overall responsibility
Heinz stands behind the entire chain, including where partners in the country of origin are involved.

International standards guide us as well: the WHO Global Code of Practice on the International Recruitment of Health Personnel, the ILO core labour standards and fair recruitment guidelines, and the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights. We only recruit in countries that are not on the WHO safeguard list.
